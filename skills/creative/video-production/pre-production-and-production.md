# Pre-Production and Production

## Shot types

- **Wide/establishing** — sets location/context.
- **Medium** — waist-up, standard dialogue coverage.
- **Close-up** — emotional emphasis.
- **Over-the-shoulder** — conversational coverage; keeps both parties' geography
  clear across cuts.
- **Two-shot** — both subjects in frame, used to show a relationship or interaction.

## Camera movement

- **Pan / tilt** — camera rotates on a fixed axis (horizontal / vertical).
- **Dolly / tracking** — camera physically moves with the subject.
- **Crane** — vertical/sweeping movement, usually for scale or a reveal.
- **Handheld** — intentional instability for immediacy or urgency; a deliberate
  choice, not a default to fall back on when a shot isn't working.

## Exposure

The exposure triangle — aperture (depth of field), shutter speed (motion
blur/strobing), ISO (sensor sensitivity/noise) — trade off against each other.
Locking frame rate and shutter angle (the 180-degree rule: shutter speed ≈ 2× frame
rate) keeps motion looking natural and consistent across a whole shoot.

## Lighting

Three-point lighting: **key** (primary light, defines the look), **fill** (softens
the shadow the key creates, doesn't erase it), **back/rim** (separates the subject
from the background). The key-to-fill ratio sets the mood — close to even (low
contrast, high-key look) for commercial and sitcom-style work, a wider ratio (more
contrast, low-key look) for drama. Color temperature must match across all three
lights, or be deliberately mismatched for an intentional effect — mixed,
*unintentional* color temperature is one of the most common amateur-looking
mistakes.

## Audio

Get this right during the shoot — audio is far harder to fix in post than to record
cleanly in the first place. Set levels against the loudest expected moment, not the
average, so peaks don't clip. Use lav mics for consistent dialogue proximity,
shotgun/boom for flexibility without visible mic hardware, both together when the
scene allows it as a backup. Before rolling: confirm levels, confirm there's no
electrical hum or HVAC noise in the room, and record a few seconds of room tone (the
space's ambient silence) for use in post — it's nearly impossible to recreate later
once the set is gone.
